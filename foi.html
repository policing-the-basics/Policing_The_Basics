Freedom of Information — what Surrey's own answers show

Seven concerns raised by Surrey Police's own FOI answers

Over 2025 and 2026, Surrey Police, the Surrey Police and Crime Commissioner's office and the IOPC answered dozens of Freedom of Information requests about complaints, evidence, arrests and domestic abuse safeguarding. Read together, the answers raise seven concerns. Each one below is drawn from the bodies' own words and figures, with the FOI reference given so it can be checked.

CONCERN 01

Surrey Police's statements about what information it holds have turned out to be wrong.

"1,000+ hours" → answered in 6 weeks

Surrey Police refused a request on cost grounds, saying the records couldn't be searched. Six weeks later it produced the same kind of data case by case. Similar contradictions appear on MARAC audits and on deleted footage.

How do we know this?

In March 2025 Surrey Police refused to say how many complaints it had suspended under Regulation 40 (the power to pause a complaint while a criminal case runs). It said Reg 40 cases were "not coded or tagged", that 12,114 records would need checking by hand, and that this would take over 1,000 hours. In April 2025, answering a new request, it listed every suspended case from January 2024 with dates, allegation types and IOPC involvement. It later produced a full table of 132 cases going back to 2021.

The same pattern appears elsewhere. Surrey Police said it holds no audit of whether officers refer high-risk domestic abuse cases to MARAC. Yet the MARAC intranet page it disclosed in the same response says "a recent audit revealed many cases are not being referred to MARAC". It refused to count how much body-worn video was deleted after complaints, saying complaint files would need reading by hand. In another response it confirmed that its deletion logs are searchable by date, incident reference, user and reason.

CONCERN 02

Surrey Police does not record the information it would need to know whether domestic abuse victims are being harmed by its processes.

No field. No audit. No record.

Its complaints database has no field for whether a complainant is a domestic abuse victim. It holds no audit of missed MARAC referrals. Missed referrals aren't recorded unless they lead to disciplinary action.

How do we know this?

Asked to show which suspended complaints involved people also recorded as domestic abuse victims, Surrey Police said there is no data field on the Professional Standards database to capture this. It cannot tell whether its complaint suspensions fall disproportionately on victims.

On MARAC, the safeguarding meetings for high-risk domestic abuse victims, Surrey Police said no reviews are carried out specifically on referral compliance. It added that missed referrals that don't meet the threshold for formal or disciplinary action "would not be recorded". It is now building an automatic referral system with a weekly audit of missed cases, but says it holds no business case or problem statement explaining why.

Meanwhile the police share of MARAC referrals in Surrey has roughly halved, while the number of cases has grown.

CONCERN 03

There is no published procedure for keeping footage once a complaint is made, and custody CCTV is overwritten after 31 days.

30 days BWV · 31 days custody CCTV

Body-worn video is deleted automatically after 30 days unless someone saves it. Custody CCTV is overwritten after 31 days, and no record of deletions is kept. Surrey Police has never shown a procedure that makes sure complaint evidence is saved in time.

How do we know this?

Surrey Police told us custody CCTV is "either downloaded or overwritten as per the 31 day retention CCTV script", and that it does not keep a record of deletions. Its Body Worn Video Policy says footage not marked as evidence is deleted after 30 days. Its BWV system logged around a million deletion or overwrite events a year in 2023–2025.

The BWV policy does say the Chief Constable has a statutory duty to preserve evidence relating to complaints. But that sentence sits inside the section on deaths and serious injuries, and no procedure follows it: nothing says who must save footage when a complaint arrives, by when, or how that is checked. Nothing published covers custody CCTV and complaints at all.

This matters most when a complaint is suspended. IOPC statutory guidance says that even then, steps should be taken to secure evidence such as CCTV that may otherwise be lost. With 30–31 day deletion and no procedure, that can't reliably happen.

CONCERN 04

Complaints against the police are frozen for long periods, with almost no outside check.

132 suspended · 11 reviewed by the IOPC

Between January 2021 and April 2025, Surrey Police suspended 132 complaints under Regulation 40. Some stayed frozen for years. Only 11 were ever taken to the IOPC.

How do we know this?

Regulation 40 lets a force pause a complaint if continuing would prejudice a criminal investigation or trial. The IOPC says this is a high threshold: there must be a specific, identified and significant prejudice. In January 2025 the IOPC warned forces that its caseworkers had seen several suspensions with no such prejudice identified.

Surrey Police's own case table shows long suspensions, many still live, and very few reviewed externally. About 50 of the 132 cases involve domestic-abuse-type allegations such as coercive control, stalking, harassment or breach of a restraining order. The data does not say who was accused in those cases (see Concern 2).

Asked about oversight, the Police and Crime Commissioner's office told the Police and Crime Panel in June 2025 that its Head of Complaints had "not identified any concerns for the use of Reg 40". Surrey Police says Reg 40 cases aren't tagged in its system, so it is unclear what that scrutiny examined.

CONCERN 05

Up to 14,000 Surrey arrests a year could be unlawful, and Surrey Police has never checked.

14,307 arrests cite "prompt and effective investigation"

The High Court has twice found Surrey arrests unlawful where "prompt and effective investigation" was relied on without real, case-specific reasons. In 2025 the arresting officer's recorded reason was: "You know how it goes, prompt and effective." Surrey cites that reason in about 1,200 arrests a month. It only "expects" officers to add specific reasons, and it has never audited whether they do. Nobody else is checking either: the Police and Crime Commissioner says it has "no evidence" of a problem, and the police watchdog holds no criteria or audits for it.

How do we know this?

Under PACE Code G, an arrest must be necessary, not just lawful. "Prompt and effective investigation" is one of the permitted reasons, but the courts have made clear that saying it isn't enough. The officer needs real reasons that fit the particular case, including why a voluntary interview wouldn't work. "Necessary" does not mean "desirable" or "convenient". Code G itself (para 4.1) also requires the arresting officer to record the reason or reasons why the arrest was necessary, and in both cases below the court judged the arrest largely on what was recorded at the time.

Two High Court cases against Surrey Police show what happens when that doesn't happen. In TL (2017), a man who had arranged to attend a voluntary interview was arrested outside Guildford police station. The court found the officer had decided to arrest him weeks earlier, "regardless of the merits", and that bail conditions were imposed "on general policy grounds rather than the particular circumstances". The arrest, bail conditions and search were declared unlawful. In ALK (2025), two parents were each held for over seven hours. The officer's reason, recorded on body-worn video, was "You know how it goes, prompt and effective". The court found both arrests unlawful: police had given no real thought to a voluntary interview, and "merely referring to the need to protect children and to protect the integrity of the investigation was not and is not enough".

Against that background, Surrey Police says only that custody officers are "expected" to make sure this reason is backed by the specific part of Code G that applies. It says no internal reviews or reports have been carried out on its use. We don't yet know how many of the 14,307 arrests had nothing more than the stock phrase recorded. A request asking exactly that went unanswered for over a year and is now with the Information Commissioner; a fresh request is due by 24 December 2026. In our view, given the case law, specific reasons should be required, not merely expected, and their use should be audited.

Nobody else is checking either. In October 2025 we sent the Office of the Police and Crime Commissioner for Surrey the "expected" answer and both judgments. Its Head of Complaints replied that "there is currently no evidence before the PCC to suggest systemic misuse of arrest powers by Surrey Police", said Code G concerns should go to Surrey's own Professional Standards Department or the IOPC, and said the office would not correspond further. We then asked the Independent Office for Police Conduct (IOPC) under FOI what it uses to judge this. The only guidance it sent was about suspending complaints; none of it covered Code G. It holds no training or written criteria on when "prompt and effective investigation" is backed by case-specific facts, and no audits or reviews of whether forces rely on boilerplate wording.

What this doesn't show
The court cases concern individual arrests; neither court made findings about Surrey's practice as a whole or ordered it to change. Citing "prompt and effective investigation" is lawful where real reasons support it, so the 14,307 figure is the number of arrests that could be affected, not a count of unlawful arrests. The PCC's view came in a reply to correspondence, not an FOI answer. The IOPC handles complaints and is not the body that inspects how forces arrest people; His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary (HMICFRS) inspects custody, and we have not yet asked it.

CONCERN 06

Requests and complaints about these issues are delayed, refused or labelled "vexatious" rather than answered.

7–8 months late · "vexatious" · "abuse of process"

Several requests took seven to eight months to answer. One got no reply at all. The Police and Crime Commissioner's office refused a request about its own training and complaints as "vexatious", and treated a complaint about Reg 40 oversight as an "abuse of process".

How do we know this?

The law requires an answer within 20 working days. Requests to Surrey Police made in February 2025 about custody CCTV, complaint evidence and arrest necessity were answered on 1 October 2025. The Information Commissioner has criticised Surrey Police's FOI compliance publicly before.

The Police and Crime Commissioner's office says that, since September 2023, it has received about 31 complaints alleging systemic problems in Surrey Police. It referred them all to Surrey Police's own Professional Standards Department and upheld none itself (it says this isn't its role). About 11 were handled under its "unreasonable and unacceptable complainants" policy. One FOI request about its staff training and complaints was refused as vexatious, and is now with the Information Commissioner.

CONCERN 07

The cost of getting it wrong is rising.

£110k → £408k a year

Surrey Police's civil claim payouts nearly quadrupled between 2021/22 and 2023/24. False imprisonment claims settled rose from 3 to 12 a year. Strip searches of women and girls nearly doubled between 2022 and 2024, with no policy change after the Baird report.

How do we know this?

Payouts are an imperfect measure. A single large claim can move the total, and settlements aren't admissions of liability. But the rise in false imprisonment and unlawful arrest claims fits the other concerns on this page.

After Dame Vera Baird KC's 2024 report on the treatment of women in Greater Manchester Police custody, Surrey Police held two meetings and circulated recommendations "where operationally practicable". It says there was no change to policy, no new or amended training, and no external audit.

Putting it together

What the FOI answers support — and what they don't

  1. Surrey Police's statements about what it holds can't be relied on. Three refusals or "not held" answers were contradicted by its own later disclosures (Concern 1).
  2. Surrey Police does not record whether complainants are domestic abuse victims, and does not audit missed MARAC referrals. It cannot know whether its processes are failing victims (Concern 2).
  3. Footage is deleted after 30–31 days, and no published procedure ensures complaint evidence is saved first (Concern 3).
  4. Complaints can be frozen for long periods with very little outside review (Concern 4).
  5. Up to 14,000 arrests a year cite a justification the High Court has twice found insufficient on its own in Surrey cases, and Surrey has never audited its use. The PCC says it has "no evidence" of a problem and the IOPC holds no criteria or audits for checking (Concern 5).
  6. Requests and complaints on these subjects are delayed, refused or labelled vexatious (Concern 6).
  7. Payouts for false imprisonment and unlawful arrest are rising (Concern 7).
What this doesn't prove: none of this shows that any particular arrest, suspension or deletion was wrong, or that anyone acted in bad faith. Several of the figures have innocent explanations that Surrey Police could give. What the answers do show is that Surrey Police often doesn't record, audit or publish the things that would let anyone, including Surrey Police itself, check. That is the gap these requests are trying to close. Corrections are welcome and will be shown, not hidden.
