Line of inquiry 02

Surrey's female arrest share stands out. Its charging pattern doesn't.

For Violence Against the Person offences, Surrey Police arrests a significantly higher share of women than four comparable neighbouring forces. That pattern disappears almost entirely by the time cases reach a charging decision — Surrey's charge-stage figures sit squarely within the normal regional range.

● Verified against raw MoJ/Home Office data — 1 Aug 2026

The headline finding

In plain terms: something in Surrey's arrest decisions for VATP produces a higher female share than in neighbouring forces. Whatever that is, it is not present — or not detectable — once those cases reach the charging stage.

Surrey has run slightly above the national female VATP arrest share since at least 2019/20 — a small, fairly stable gap of around 1 to 1.6 percentage points. What's new is the widening. Starting in 2022/23, the gap more than doubles and keeps growing, reaching 4.6 percentage points by 2024/25. That timing lines up closely with Surrey Police's "Our Plan," launched October 2023, which explicitly drove up arrests including for domestic abuse and VAWG offending — see the Full Picture for the documented policy context.

Reading the two stages together

What changes between arrest and charge
● At arrest

Surrey is the clear outlier. Its female VATP arrest share is significantly higher than all four comparator forces — not just the England & Wales average, but forces with broadly similar populations, geography, and policing context.

● At charge

Surrey looks unremarkable. Its female VATP charge share sits inside the normal range shown by Hampshire, Sussex, Kent, and Thames Valley — none of the differences are statistically distinguishable from noise.

What this rules out

If Surrey prosecuted women more readily in general — a broad, force-wide charging culture — you'd expect the charge-stage figures to look unusual too. They don't. That points away from a general prosecutorial pattern and toward something specific to the point of arrest: how officers assess and respond to incidents involving women, particularly in the kind of ambiguous, multi-party incidents common to domestic abuse call-outs.

What this doesn't establish

Caveat

This is a statistical pattern requiring explanation, not evidence of unfair or unlawful treatment. It is consistent with several different underlying causes — including arrest decisions that are appropriately cautious in ambiguous domestic incidents, as well as arrest decisions that are not. The gap between arrest and charge stage is the thing that needs explaining; this data cannot explain it on its own. See the MARAC referral gap page for the related line of inquiry this feeds into.

Methodology note

How these figures were produced
Sourcing

Arrest-stage figures: Home Office "Arrests open data tables," year ending March 2025 — row-level arrest records, filtered to reason_for_arrest containing "Violence against the person," aggregated by police force area, financial year, and sex.

Charge-stage figures: MoJ "Outcomes by Offence" data tool, raw CSV export underlying the published pivot table — row-level prosecution records, filtered to Offence Group "01: Violence against the person," aggregated by police force area, calendar year, and sex.

Significance testing: two-proportion z-test, Surrey vs. each comparator force, using the raw female/male counts for the most recent available year at each stage.

Every aggregate figure on this page was independently recomputed from row-level source data rather than taken from a pre-built pivot summary, and cross-checked against figures separately extracted via the published pivot tools. The two methods agreed exactly.
