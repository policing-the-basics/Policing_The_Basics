FINDING 01

Women are arrested at a higher rate in Surrey than in similar neighbouring areas — for violence offences specifically. And the gap is new.

+1.1pp → +4.6pp since 2021/22

Surrey has run slightly above the national female VATP arrest share since at least 2019/20, but that gap was small and stable. Since 2022/23 it has more than tripled, reaching 23.8% vs 19.1% nationally by 2024/25 — statistically significant against every comparable neighbouring force.

How do we know this?

We compared Surrey's arrest figures against the England & Wales average, and then separately against four similar neighbouring forces (Hampshire, Sussex, Kent, Thames Valley) to rule out the possibility that Surrey is simply different from cities or very different regions. In every comparison, Surrey's female arrest share for this offence category came out higher, and the gap has been widening since 2022/23.

Surrey has actually run slightly above the national average since at least 2019/20 — but that gap was small and stable for years (around 1 to 1.6 percentage points). What's new is that it started widening sharply from 2022/23 onward, more than doubling by 2023/24. That timing lines up closely with "Our Plan," Surrey Police's own strategic plan launched October 2023 (see Finding 3).

Importantly, this gap mostly disappears by the time cases reach a charging decision — Surrey's charge-stage figures are statistically indistinguishable from its neighbours. Something specific to the point of arrest, not general policing culture, appears to be driving this.

FINDING 02

Surrey Police refers far fewer domestic abuse cases to the safeguarding process (MARAC) than police elsewhere.

49.1% vs 63.6%

Share of MARAC referrals made by the police, Surrey vs. England & Wales, year ending March 2025 — a 14.4 percentage point gap.

How do we know this?

MARAC exists to protect domestic abuse victims judged to be at high risk of serious harm. Police are normally the largest single source of referrals into that process. In Surrey, police account for a noticeably smaller share of referrals than the national pattern — meaning either fewer high-risk cases are being identified by officers, or identified cases aren't being referred.

When we asked Surrey Police directly whether any audit exists to catch cases that should have been referred but weren't, the answer was: no information held. There is currently no known mechanism to catch a missed referral.

FINDING 03

Surrey Police deliberately increased arrests, including for domestic abuse, from October 2023.

"Our Plan" — Oct 2023

Surrey Police's own Force Management Statement states arrests rose "most prominently" after this strategic plan launched, with tackling Violence Against Women and Girls named as an explicit objective.

How do we know this?

This isn't inferred — it's Surrey Police's own public statement. Their Force Management Statement 2024–2025 explicitly credits "Our Plan," launched October 2023, with driving up arrests, and names tackling VAWG as one of two headline missions.

This is a legitimate, publicly declared policing priority, not evidence of wrongdoing on its own. But a genuine crackdown on VAWG offending should mostly increase arrests of male perpetrators, since most domestic abuse offending is male-on-female. If the female arrest share rose specifically alongside it, that points toward a broader "arrest more readily" shift that may not have distinguished victims from perpetrators as carefully as it should.

FINDING 04

National guidance told every MARAC area to build a "counter allegations" process into their protocol. Surrey's current protocol has none.

Sept 2023 → March 2025

SafeLives — the national body behind MARAC — issued this instruction 18 months before Surrey's most recent protocol update. The gap is directly checkable in Surrey's own published document.

How do we know this?

Counter allegations happen when both people in an incident accuse each other. Misidentifying who the real victim is in these situations is a recognised national problem — serious enough that SafeLives published dedicated guidance in September 2023, specifically instructing local areas to add a "counter allegations pathway" to their MARAC operating protocol.

Surrey's MARAC Protocol was updated in March 2025 — eighteen months later. We read it in full. It covers referral criteria, risk assessment, information sharing, and governance in detail. It does not mention counter allegations, primary aggressor identification, or anything addressing this specific, named national instruction.

This is the clearest, most directly checkable documentary gap in the whole picture — it doesn't rely on statistics or inference at all.

FINDING 05

This isn't a fringe theory — it's a recognised, actively studied national problem.

"the 5 M's"

Peer-reviewed research and national campaigning reports confirm domestic abuse victims being wrongly arrested via counter-allegations is a known, current, and worsening issue in England & Wales policing.

How do we know this?

A 2025 academic study (Barlow & Walklate, funded by the N8 Policing Research Partnership) interviewed both frontline officers and domestic abuse victim-survivors about exactly this problem. Officers themselves described cases of women being wrongly arrested. The researchers identified five recurring factors — manipulation, misidentification, miscommunication, misunderstanding, and minimisation — that combine to produce these outcomes.

A separate 2026 report found that among domestic abuse victims supported by one national charity, 30–40% had either been threatened with arrest or criminalised themselves.

Putting it together

What the evidence supports — and what it doesn't

  1. Surrey arrests a higher share of women for violence offences than similar areas, and the gap is growing (Finding 1).
  2. That gap is concentrated in adults, not children — the age range where domestic abuse is most common (Finding 1).
  3. The gap mostly disappears by charge stage, suggesting something specific happens at the point of arrest, not throughout the whole justice process (Finding 1).
  4. Surrey's own safeguarding referral rate for domestic abuse is well below the national rate, and no mechanism exists to catch missed cases (Finding 2).
  5. Surrey Police openly pushed to increase arrests, including for domestic abuse, from October 2023 (Finding 3).
  6. National guidance said every area needed a counter-allegations process by 2023. Surrey's current protocol, updated 18 months later, doesn't have one (Finding 4).
  7. This exact combination — arrest-first pressure without robust primary-aggressor guidance — is a documented, actively researched national problem, not a Surrey-specific theory (Finding 5).
What this doesn't prove: none of this establishes that any individual arrest was wrong, or that Surrey Police is acting in bad faith. Surrey's arrest increase was a public, legitimate strategic choice. What the evidence supports is a specific, answerable question: has that arrest-first push, combined with a documented gap in counter-allegation guidance, resulted in domestic abuse victims being wrongly arrested in Surrey more often than it should? That question is not yet answered — it's the question two pending Freedom of Information requests, tracked on the MARAC Gap page, are trying to get closer to.
